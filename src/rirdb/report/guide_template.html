Reverb Dataset Field Guide

An impulse response is the sound of a space answering a single click. Researchers have published millions of them. This guide lists every public collection we could find, says what each licence lets you do and where to download it, and shows what the collections cover and what is still missing.

Ilias Ibnyahya · Centre for Digital Music, Queen Mary University of London · facts checked

Basics

What is in a reverb dataset?

An impulse response (IR) is made by playing a sweep or a click through a loudspeaker and recording it with a microphone; processing turns the recording into the response to one perfect click. Each IR belongs to one loudspeaker position and one microphone position, and it includes the equipment as well as the room, so moving either changes the IR. A file can hold one IR or thousands. Musicians load IRs into convolution reverbs, acousticians read room properties from them, and machine-learning models use them to make a dry recording sound as if it had been made somewhere else.

Datasets differ in four ways that matter when you choose one: how many spaces they cover and how many positions in each (a dataset can hold a hundred thousand IRs from a single room), how the sound was captured, which loudspeaker was used (an omnidirectional source, as room-acoustic standards ask, or a directional studio speaker), and what the licence allows.

Mono

One omnidirectional microphone. Enough for reverberation time, clarity and most machine-learning work.

Binaural

Two microphones in the ears of a dummy head. Plays back as three-dimensional sound on headphones.

Ambisonic

A compact microphone that also records where sound comes from. Can be played on any speaker layout, on headphones or in VR.

Microphone array

Many microphones on a line, a plane, a sphere or a grid. For sound-field research and source localisation.

Licences

What can I do with it?

A licence answers three questions. May I use the data in a commercial product? May I train a machine-learning model on it? May I share the audio with others? Most reverb datasets use a Creative Commons licence, and most of those allow all three, with credit to the authors. The exceptions are worth knowing before you build on a dataset.

How to credit a dataset

Name the dataset and its authors, link its DOI, name the licence and say whether you changed the files, for example: Arni variable-acoustics room RIRs (Aalto University), doi:10.5281/zenodo.6985104, CC BY 4.0, trimmed to 2 s. In a track or a plug-in, put this in the credits or the About box. Data under a software licence (MIT, BSD, Apache) must ship with its licence text.

What we found in the fine print

    This is a plain-language summary of the licences, not legal advice. Several questions are still open for machine learning: whether a trained model, or what it generates, is an adaptation of its training data (which matters for share-alike and attribution); whether a model trained on non-commercial data may later be used commercially; and how text-and-data-mining exceptions (UK CDPA s29A for non-commercial research; EU DSM Directive, Articles 3 and 4) apply alongside a licence. When in doubt, ask the authors; most are glad to be asked.

    Can I use it?

    Licences that leave questions

    state no licence, give contradictory terms, and only two licences (EchoThief, SoundSpaces) say anything explicit about AI. Of the spaces we analysed, only come from datasets whose licence allows a commercial model; the MIT IR Survey's 270 places have no licence and EchoThief forbids AI training.

    HelpsA standard licence with its version (CC BY 4.0 or CC0 are the simplest to reuse), one sentence on machine-learning use saying whether a trained model counts as an adaptation, and the same statement in the record, the README and the files.

    Links that rot

    The OpenAIR website went offline in 2026 (its files survive on a University of York file server), the original ACE Challenge site is unreachable, UCL's server for the eBrIRD hearing-aid BRIRs did not respond when we checked, the Fokke van Saane collection exists only in the Wayback Machine, and the DRR-scaled BRIR archive on Zenodo is truncated. only on a lab, company or personal website, some through Google Drive or Dropbox links.

    HelpsA repository that issues DOIs and keeps checksums, and a second copy of anything hosted on a personal page.

    What does it cover?

    Many IRs, few rooms

    The room IRs we indexed come from spaces, and of them come from three single-room laboratory datasets (). Another IRs (MIRACLE) were measured in an anechoic chamber and hold no reverberation. Training on "millions of IRs" can mean training on a handful of rooms.

    HelpsBreadth over density: many rooms, each measured at several source and receiver positions, as OK5 does for 25 spaces of one building. The MIT IR Survey covers 270 everyday places, but with one IR each.

    Halls, churches and other large spaces

    Half of the measured rooms have a reverberation time below ; only of ring longer than 2 s. Concert halls (), theatres and auditoria () and places of worship () are a small share of the spaces, yet they are the rooms music is made for.

    HelpsMeasurements of performance venues and worship spaces across stage and audience positions, with full-length decays.

    Whose rooms?

    of the datasets with a known origin were measured or made in Europe and in North America. The performance spaces of most of the world's musical cultures are not in any public dataset.

    HelpsMeasurement campaigns and partnerships beyond Europe and North America, starting with the venues where music is played there.

    Real life is not an empty, still room

    Few datasets cover vehicles ( spaces), rooms with people in them (SoundCam), changing furniture (Motus) or sources and listeners that move (trajectoRIR, the robot and transition datasets). Panels and absorbers that change are common, but only in lab rooms (Arni, dEchorate, UPV, SRIRACHA, BRUDEX).

    HelpsOccupancy, doors, furniture and movement recorded as variables, with the same positions measured under each condition.

    Rooms without a description

    Room volume is published for of the spaces. Only datasets publish a full 3-D model or scan of their rooms, and only list the surface materials in full. Without them an IR cannot be tied to the room that made it, which simulation, inverse problems and room-aware generative models all need.

    HelpsDimensions or a floor plan, volume, materials and photos for every room, and a 3-D model where possible.

    Can I trust it and learn from it?

    Files that are not what they seem

    We found IRs cut before the decay ends (TAU-SRIR about 0.3 s, UPV 0.37 s, C4DM 2 s for a 2.4 s hall), content that stops at 12 to 16 kHz inside 48 kHz files (dEchorate), byte-identical duplicates in TAU-SRIR, and counts that do not match the files (dataset lists cite 344 IRs for the Aachen AIR database, whose release 1.4 holds 107 two-channel records; SRIRACHA's files hold half of the 2.65 million its abstract announces).

    HelpsPublish raw, full-length IRs at their true sampling rate, and check counts and checksums before each release.

    Splits that leak

    Most datasets measure one room at many positions, and some rooms appear in several datasets: Arni in three, the Alamire lab in MYRiAD and trajectoRIR, the Room Transition rooms again in ERTD's simulation. Split the IRs at random and the test set holds rooms the model has already heard, so the scores measure memory rather than generalisation.

    HelpsA room ID on every IR that stays the same across releases and datasets, a suggested split by room plus a held-out dataset, and a published list of duplicates and shared rooms.

    Every dataset sounds like its equipment

    In an audio embedding (CLAP) of the analysed IRs, 99% sit closest to an IR from their own dataset. Loudspeaker, microphones, sweep and processing leave a mark on every file, so a model trained on a few datasets can learn the recording chain instead of the room.

    HelpsThe loudspeaker and microphone responses and the processing steps published with the IRs, and results reported on a held-out dataset as well as on held-out positions.

    Simulation without ground truth

    Simulated datasets are large (GWA: 2 million IRs in about 19,000 rooms; SpaDenRIR: 5.3 million, first 100 ms only), but none of the large ones comes with measurements of the same rooms. Measured and simulated IRs of the same space exist only in small studies: heritage spaces (York Minster's Chapter House, Ely's Lady Chapel, Aachen's St Nicholas Chapel, Sant Climent de Taüll, three Italian Renaissance theatres), a racquetball court at Stanford, a hearing-aid lab in Nancy (BinauRec) and the two rooms of ERTD. BRAS, Motus and FLAIR publish the geometry, materials or point cloud needed to simulate their rooms. None of the simulated sets has been analysed here yet.

    HelpsSimulated and measured IRs at the same positions in many rooms, released with the geometry and materials used for the simulation, so simulators can be checked and models trained on simulation can be tested on real rooms.

    The bass end

    In the typical analysed dataset, of IRs give a valid T30 in the 63 Hz octave band and at 125 Hz, against at 1 kHz. Short files, background noise and small loudspeakers all cut off the bass. In small rooms the lowest octaves also fall below the Schroeder frequency, where a few room modes dominate and one T30 per octave says less.

    HelpsLong or averaged sweeps, a source with real output below 100 Hz, quiet conditions (ventilation off), and files long enough for the bass decay to reach the noise floor.

    Levels that cannot be compared

    None of the analysed datasets publishes a calibration or a free-field reference that holds across the set, so sound strength (G) and late lateral level, two ISO 3382-1 parameters that describe how loud and enveloping a hall is, cannot be computed from public data. Where files are normalised one by one, the level differences between positions are lost too.

    HelpsOne gain for the whole set, stated; the microphone and amplifier calibration, or a free-field measurement of the source; and the silence before the direct sound kept, so arrival times survive.

    Storing and sharing

    How to store and share reverb data

    The field does not need one giant download. It needs each dataset published so that it lasts, and one shared catalogue that points to all of them, with their licences and checksums. Audio stays where it was published; the catalogue, small enough for git, does the joining.

    A shared catalogue for the field

    How this guide's catalogue is built, and how we propose to keep it.

    • One record per dataset, readable by machines.Fill the repository's DataCite fields completely: every creator with an ORCID, the licence with its SPDX identifier, and links to the paper and to earlier versions. Add schema.org Dataset or Croissant metadata so search engines and ML tools can find the data. This guide's registry adds the three permissions in plain words and what is known about the rooms.
    • Checksums and versions for every file.Record the version DOI and a SHA-256 for every file (Zenodo lists MD5), for example in a SHA256SUMS file, so a new download can be proved identical. Data that lives only on a website can still disappear: keep a preservation copy and ask the authors to deposit it.
    • Derived data travels, audio stays.Scalar parameters and quality flags for the IRs analysed here take a few hundred MB, against 0.5 TB of audio. Publish them as Parquet with their own DOI, one file per source-licence family, each row giving its source DOI and licence. Publish embeddings, and anything derived from EchoThief, the MIT IR Survey or TAU-SRIR, only with the authors' agreement.
    • Mirror what the licence allows, link the rest.Mirror each redistributable dataset as its own record that keeps its licence, notices and authors and links the original DOI. Mirror non-commercial data for non-commercial use only and Voxengo only as the complete set; do not mirror datasets whose terms grant no distribution (EchoThief, S3A, TAU-SRIR).

    If you publish a dataset

    1. Deposit it where it gets a DOI.Zenodo, DepositOnce or your institution's repository keep the files, publish checksums and survive website moves. Zenodo takes 100 files and 50 GB per record (up to 200 GB on request): zip small files and split large sets into linked records. Data already on GitHub or Hugging Face can get a DOI there too.
    2. Choose a standard licence, with its version.CC BY 4.0 or CC0 are the simplest to reuse. Add one sentence on machine-learning use. Put the same licence in the record, the README and the files' own licence fields, such as SOFA's GLOBAL:License, whose default ("No license provided") caused one of the conflicts above.
    3. Use open formats.WAV or FLAC for single IRs; SOFA (AES69) with a standard convention (SingleRoomSRIR, SingleRoomMIMOSRIR, MultiSpeakerBRIR) for binaural, Ambisonic and array IRs with their positions; HDF5 with a documented layout for dense grids.
    4. Keep the whole decay.Do not cut, fade or denoise the IRs you publish. If you also release processed versions, publish the raw ones alongside them. Keep some noise floor after the decay so others can check it.
    5. Keep the levels.Do not normalise each file on its own: apply one gain to the whole set and state it, and publish the calibration (or a free-field measurement of the source) so that sound strength and level differences can be computed. Keep the silence before the direct sound.
    6. State the real sampling rate and bandwidth.Do not upsample. If content stops at 16 kHz, say so.
    7. One metadata row per IR.A CSV or JSON table: file, a room ID that stays the same across releases, condition, source and receiver positions and orientations, source type and directivity, microphone type, excitation signal, processing applied, background-noise level, occupancy, date, temperature and humidity. Measure some positions twice so others can estimate the uncertainty, and suggest a train/test split by room.
    8. Describe every room.Dimensions or a floor plan, volume, surface materials with their absorption per octave band where known, photos, a 3-D model if you have one, and the reverberation times you measured per octave band, with the method.
    9. Write the README as a datasheet.Why the data was made, how it was measured, what is in it (with counts that match the files), known issues, and what it should not be used for. Version the release and keep a changelog when you fix something.
    10. Let people fetch only the IRs.Split large releases into parts, as MYRiAD's 200 MB "econ" archive does next to its 31 GB full release.
    11. Prefer safe, plain files.Avoid Python pickles (loading one can run code; GTU-RIR, and MRTD before version 3, ship only a pickle) and MATLAB object files; use simple file names without spaces or brackets.

    Glossary

    Words used in this guide

    Impulse response (IR, RIR)
    The response of a space, and of the equipment, to a single instantaneous click. Convolving a dry sound with it places that sound in the space.
    BRIR
    Binaural room impulse response: an IR recorded at the two ears of a person or a dummy head.
    SRIR
    Spatial room impulse response: an IR that keeps the direction each reflection arrives from, recorded with an Ambisonic microphone or an array.
    Ambisonics (FOA, HOA)
    A way of recording the full sphere of sound. First order (FOA, B-format) uses 4 channels; higher orders (HOA) use more channels for sharper directions. A-format is the raw capsule signals.
    Microphone array
    Several microphones in a known layout: a line, a plane, a sphere such as the 32-capsule Eigenmike, or a grid scanned by a robot.
    Reverberation time (RT60, T30, T20)
    How long sound takes to fall by 60 dB once the source stops. It is read from the smoothed decay curve of the IR: T30 fits a line from −5 to −35 dB and doubles that 30 dB time; T20 fits −5 to −25 dB and triples it. ISO 3382-1 keeps the bottom of the fit at least 10 dB above the noise floor, so T30 needs 45 dB of clean decay and T20 35 dB.
    EDT
    Early decay time: the first 10 dB of the decay (0 to −10 dB), multiplied by six. Closer to how reverberant a room sounds.
    Clarity (C50, C80)
    Energy in the first 50 or 80 ms against the rest, in dB. High values sound clear and dry. C50 is used for speech, C80 for music.
    DRR
    Direct-to-reverberant ratio: how loud the direct sound is compared with everything after it. It falls as you move away from the source.
    Octave band
    A frequency range one octave wide, named by its centre: 63, 125, 250 Hz … 8 kHz. Rooms decay at different rates in each band.
    Sampling rate and bandwidth
    Samples per second (48 kHz is common). Content can stop well below half the sampling rate if the recording was resampled, or if the sweep, loudspeaker or microphone did not go higher.
    Mid frequencies
    The average of the 500 Hz and 1 kHz octave bands, the usual single-number value in ISO 3382-1.
    Definition (D50)
    The share of the sound energy that arrives in the first 50 ms. High values mean clear speech.
    STI
    Speech transmission index, from 0 (unintelligible) to 1 (perfect). Here it is computed from the IR alone, without background noise.
    Noise floor
    The level of noise left in an IR once the decay has died away, from background noise and the measuring equipment. Decay times can only be read down to about 10 dB above it.
    SOFA (AES69)
    An open file format for spatial acoustic data that stores IRs together with source and receiver positions.
    DOI
    Digital Object Identifier: a permanent link to a dataset that keeps working when a website moves.
    SPDX identifier
    A standard short name for a licence, such as CC-BY-4.0, that software can read.
    Creative Commons
    BY: credit the authors. SA: share adaptations under the same licence. NC: non-commercial use only. ND: share only unmodified copies. CC0: the authors waive their rights as far as the law allows (credit is still good practice). The version matters: 4.0 licences also cover European database rights, which most earlier versions did not.
    Dense grid
    Many closely spaced positions in one room, for studying how the sound field changes from point to point.
    Convolution reverb
    An audio effect that applies a measured IR to any sound, the most common musical use of these datasets.

    Method

    How this guide was made

    1. Registry. One YAML entry per dataset with its source, licence, capture formats, counts and what it says about its rooms.
    2. Search. In October 2026 we searched research repositories, papers, curated lists and non-English sources for datasets missing from the registry. A second, independent pass checked each new find against its own pages (repository record, paper or website) before it was added.
    3. Download and verify. were fetched. were checked against their publishers' checksums; for the whose hosts publish none, we recorded our own. Every extracted file was hashed.
    4. Index. IR records in one table: from real spaces and from anechoic chambers.
    5. Analyse. ISO 3382-1 parameters per octave band (reverberation times, clarity, definition), DRR and STI, computed with pyfar and pyrato with explicit noise-floor handling and validity rules, on IRs: all of each sparse dataset and a fixed one-in-sixteen sample of the dense ones. T30 matches OpenAIR's published tables on average (median ratio 0.99 to 1.00 from 250 Hz to 4 kHz; 69 to 76% of spaces within 10%) and the ACE Challenge ground truth (75 to 93% of IRs within 10% from 500 Hz to 2 kHz; DRR within 0.2 dB on average). EDT and C80 scatter more, as they change from seat to seat. Parameters come from the omnidirectional channel where there is one, otherwise from a dummy-head ear or one capsule of a spherical array, so the early parameters of those datasets are not strictly ISO 3382-1 values. STI is computed from the IR alone, without background noise.
    6. Audit. The analysed IRs of real spaces were checked for truncation, upsampling, duplicates, tonal noise, sweep residue and dropouts.

    What the counts mean. An IR record is one source position heard at one receiver position: the channels of a binaural or Ambisonic capture count once, while dense single-microphone grids count every microphone. A space is a room, hall or outdoor site as its dataset names it; a variable-acoustics room counts once, whatever its configurations. Kinds of space are assigned from names and checked by hand where the names misled the rules; a room indexed under several datasets (the ACE rooms, the Arni room) counts once. The ACE Challenge's single-microphone and array recordings are one entry here.

    Licences. Licence terms are as stated by the publishers' records, checked on . The three permissions are a plain-language reading, not legal advice.

    Code and data. The pipeline (rirdb), the registry and the tables behind this page are in github.com/ilias-audio/room-impulse-responses. To report an error or a missing dataset, open an issue there.
